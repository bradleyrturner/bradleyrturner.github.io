**Cultivating Outsiderness: How Activist Short Sellers Turn Outsider Identity Into a Resource** *(Job Market Paper)*  
*With Georg Rilinger*  

<details>
  <summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
  <p>Actors on the periphery of institutional fields offer an important source of
  innovation but risk being dismissed for lack of legitimacy. To gain influence,
  outsiders are expected to acquire credentials, forge ties to incumbents, and
  translate their claims into the mainstream's idiom. Many activist short sellers
  selectively do the opposite. Drawing on 25 interviews, a 40-year case study of
  the occupation (1985–2024), and a corpus of 830 short reports, I show that these
  actors cultivate outsider identity markers—anonymity, absurd pseudonyms,
  moralized language—alongside select insider markers of rigor and credentials.
  The result is what I call the <em>institutionalized stranger</em>: a durable
  position that fuses insider competence with outsider license, enabling short
  sellers to investigate, publish on, and profit from corporate fraud that
  insiders are structurally unable to expose.</p>
</details>

**The Art of the Short Story: Second-Order Inference and Rhetorical Fit in Strategic Communication** *(working title)*    
*With Georg Rilinger (equal authorship)*  

Scholars tend to view narratives as heuristics that reduce complexity. Clear, consistent, and well-supported stories are seen as most effective in shaping market valuation. Yet activist short sellers, financial-market contrarians who depend on rapid market revaluations, often publish layered, rhetorically complex reports with mixed evidence. In this paper we explore why, leveraging qualitative coding and machine learning to analyze 830 reports, as well as financial data, social and mainstream media response, and interviews with activist short sellers. We theorize about strategic communication under uncertainty and contribute to research on classification, valuation, and narrative.

**Stories In Stories: Why Anecdotes Strengthen or Undermine Entrepreneurs' Credibility** *(working title)*    

This paper contributes to a debate about the efficacy of strategic storytelling for organizations and entrepreneurs. Leveraging qualitative analysis of 100 entrepreneur-investor interactions at three pitch events, I focus on the concrete anecdotes in pitches, analyzing the symbolic and persuasive work they accomplish, and how investors respond to them. I find that not all entrepreneurs tell stories, not all stories resonate with investors, and even resonant stories can be dismissed as irrelevant for the investment decision. Based on the lines of questions that entrepreneurs receive from investors, I hypothesize that the familiar Aha! moments and origin stories are less compelling than more distinctive, detailed, concrete stories about personal commitment, customer problems, and product trials. Building on existing research, I theorize about why stories strengthen versus undermine credibility, arguing that it depends on whether investors perceive them as complements or substitutes for data and argument claims. By unpacking the high-level concept of storytelling, and analyzing the full range of investor responses to it, I contribute to research on entrepreneurship, impression management, and narrative.

## Publications

**[Narrative Affordances: What Stories Can and Cannot Do](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/annals.2023.0101)**  
*Academy of Management Annals*, 2025  

details>
<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
<p>I review four decades of research on narrative, or story, integrating theoretical and
empirical approaches that influence management, strategy, and organization theory. I
conceptualize narrative as a tool of thinking and communicating that offers particular
possibilities for action, or affordances. Narrative affordances derive from narrative&rsquo;s
formal structure and the social norms that affect its use. I inductively identify four
actions or affordances that individuals and organizations advance with narrative: making
meaning, persuading, sharing, and coordinating. Together, the interdependent affordances
articulate a programmatic theory of narrative. Clustering by action reveals how narrative
research programs fit or clash as a function of the affordances they emphasize. I also
highlight how narrative can be compared to and combined with other conceptual and
linguistic tools, like quantification. Overall, this review highlights canonical and
cutting-edge work, finds common cause across strands of research, and concludes with
promising paths forward from the knowledge frontier for both management and narrative
research.</p>
</details>

## Under Review

**Cultivating Outsiderness: How Activist Short Sellers Turn Outsider Identity Into a Resource** *(Job Market Paper)*  
*With Georg Rilinger*  

<details>
  <summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
  <p>Actors on the periphery of institutional fields offer an important source of
  innovation but risk being dismissed for lack of legitimacy. To gain influence,
  outsiders are expected to acquire credentials, forge ties to incumbents, and
  translate their claims into the mainstream's idiom. Many activist short sellers
  selectively do the opposite. Drawing on 25 interviews, a 40-year case study of
  the occupation (1985–2024), and a corpus of 830 short reports, I show that these
  actors cultivate outsider identity markers—anonymity, absurd pseudonyms,
  moralized language—alongside select insider markers of rigor and credentials.
  The result is what I call the <em>institutionalized stranger</em>: a durable
  position that fuses insider competence with outsider license, enabling short
  sellers to investigate, publish on, and profit from corporate fraud that
  insiders are structurally unable to expose.</p>
</details>

## Preparing for Submission

**The Art of the Short Story: Second-Order Inference and Rhetorical Fit in Strategic Communication**
*With Georg Rilinger (equal authorship)*  

Why do activist short sellers publish sprawling, theatrical reports, even though
existing theory suggests that focused, conventional narratives are best suited to
coordinate the actions of investors? 

<details>
<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
<p>To answer this question, our abductive study
combines computational analysis of 830 reports targeting U.S.-listed firms
(2008&ndash;2024) with 21 practitioner interviews, market data, and audience reception
evidence. The corpus reveals that report architecture tracks the evaluative
environment: tone reflects house style, but rhetorical breadth rises with evaluative
uncertainty and, among broad reports, narrative integration rises with pre-campaign
volatility, our indicator of active contestation. Interviews reveal the logic behind
these patterns. Sellers anticipate not how &ldquo;the market&rdquo; will respond
(third-order inference) but how specific audiences will react in sequence
(second-order inference). When audiences share an evaluative standard, the two
coincide and focused reports suffice. When standards diverge, modular reports make
competing standards and their audiences visible; when uncertainty coincides with
active contestation, integrated reports bind these lines into an overarching
narrative that is easier to grasp and transmit. Both departures sacrifice genre
conformity, so sellers reserve them for audiences that focus cannot coordinate.
Comparing our explanation&mdash;rhetorical fit&mdash;with accepted alternatives, we
develop a more general account of coordination in valuation processes: the focused
signal of focal-point theory is the special case of aligned standards, and rhetorical
complexity enables second-order inference where shared standards are absent.</p>
</details>

**Stories In Stories: Why Anecdotes Strengthen or Undermine Entrepreneurs' Credibility** *(working title)*    

<details>
<summary><strong>Abstract</strong></summary>
<p>This paper contributes to a debate about the efficacy of strategic storytelling for organizations and entrepreneurs. Leveraging qualitative analysis of 100 entrepreneur-investor interactions at three pitch events, I focus on the concrete anecdotes in pitches, analyzing the symbolic and persuasive work they accomplish, and how investors respond to them. I find that not all entrepreneurs tell stories, not all stories resonate with investors, and even resonant stories can be dismissed as irrelevant for the investment decision. Based on the lines of questions that entrepreneurs receive from investors, I hypothesize that the familiar Aha! moments and origin stories are less compelling than more distinctive, detailed, concrete stories about personal commitment, customer problems, and product trials. Building on existing research, I theorize about why stories strengthen versus undermine credibility, arguing that it depends on whether investors perceive them as complements or substitutes for data and argument claims. By unpacking the high-level concept of storytelling, and analyzing the full range of investor responses to it, I contribute to research on entrepreneurship, impression management, and narrative.</p>
</details>
